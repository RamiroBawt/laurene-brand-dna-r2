Which of these is actually you?

Four visual languages for the same brand voice. Same palette, same type stack, same Compass frame, same content mix. What differs is how each language renders that content visually — type-led data architecture, intimate warmth, publication-system rigor, or photography-dominant editorial. The decision is which visual register feels like you.

Option A — The Market-Insider Brief

Data is the headline.

What it commits to

Information density. Every slide carries a fact, a comparison, or a market specific. Photography exists to anchor data, not set mood.

What it rules out

Mood-first photography. Pinyon Script as a structural element. Anything that doesn't earn its place with specificity.

The reader this attracts

The researching buyer — relocating families cross-referencing schools, retirees doing due diligence, second-home buyers who hire after the homework.

Option B — The Intimate Recommender

The person you'd trust to pick a restaurant.

What it commits to

Personal warmth as the authority signal. Italic-color-accent serif headlines. Pinyon Script woven through as warmth markers. Candlelit photography. Laurène in frame.

What it rules out

Forensic data density. Impersonal taxonomy. Cold editorial restraint. Anything that hides the person.

The reader this attracts

The feel-first buyer who hires on taste and trust — the person who calls because they liked you, not because they vetted you.

See in the mocks

Notice the listing-carousel-1 cover — the italic-color-accent serif headline and the Pinyon Script signature. That's the philosophy expressing itself.

Option C — The Positioned Expert

The format itself is the authority.

What it commits to

Publication-system rigor. Masthead-rule on every slide. Kicker-tab, folio, edition stamp, imprint strip — fixed positions. The consistency itself is the signature.

What it rules out

Improvisational visual variety. Mood-driven warmth that overrides format. Casual register. Anything that breaks the publishing rhythm.

The reader this attracts

The standard-setter — the buyer who notices the brand is different and that's why they call.

See in the mocks

Notice the listing-carousel-1 cover — pure typographic, no photo. The restraint is the publication signature.

Option D — The Editorial Lens

The photograph bears the weight.

What it commits to

Photography as the dominant visual element on every slide. Typography lives in peripheral positions — eyebrow top-left, caption bottom-left, folio top-right. Restraint in type, generosity in image.

What it rules out

Type-led hero treatment. Abstract design moves. Stock photography that could appear in any realtor's feed. The photo carries the weight or the slide doesn't work.

The reader this attracts

The buyer who reads the brand visually before they read the words — drawn in by composition, photography, and physical specificity.

See in the mocks

Notice the listing-carousel-1 cover — the photo is the cover. Typography is small, peripheral, only labeling. Closer to a Sotheby's listing book than a realtor feed.

This is a foundational decision — not a visual preference. Once you pick a direction, the content pipeline, the caption voice, the photography brief, and the production calendar all align behind it. That's why this choice matters more than "which palette" or "how much script."

There's no wrong answer here. A, B, C, and D are genuinely different visual languages for the same content, not a ranking. Pick the one that made you uncomfortable in a productive way.
